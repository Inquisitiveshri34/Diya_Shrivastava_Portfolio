## 4. Case study: ClauseCheck
 
Every featured case study follows the same order. All facts, figures and roles were checked against each project's repository and Git history.
 
### Header
 
- **Label:** *Legal & procurement · Sep–Oct 2026*
- **Title:** *ClauseCheck — Procurement RAG Assistant*
- **Summary:** *An assistant that answers plain-English questions about supplier contracts, backs every answer with the exact clause it came from, checks every figure against that clause — and refuses when the documents don't say.*
- **Details:** *Group project (team of 2) · I built the document and retrieval pipeline — sourcing, loading and cleaning, chunking, the evaluation question set, vector and keyword search, fusion and re-ranking — then led integration and debugging, and built versions 2 and 3, including the redesigned interface and figure checking. My teammate built the answer generation, relevance gate, citation validator, API and first frontend.*
- **Stack:** *Python, ChromaDB, BM25, Sentence-Transformers, Groq, Gemini, SQLite, FastAPI, Streamlit*
- **Context:** *Built during a simulated industry sprint.*
- **Links:** *View code* → [GitHub](https://github.com/kalviumcommunity/S66-0926-DY-RAG-ClauseCheck)
### At a glance
 
Retrieval and refusal figures are from the 40-question tuning set; see Limitations.
 
| Number | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 93.5% recall@5 | The correct clause was in the top 5 results for 29 of 31 answerable questions |
| 9 of 9 refused | Every question the contracts can't answer was refused rather than guessed |
| 1 of 31 wrongly refused | A 3.2% false-refusal rate on answerable questions |
| 158 documents | From 69 suppliers, split into 8,062 clause-level chunks |
| 375 tests | Passing in the final version, up from 262 at version 1 |
| 1–5 s | Typical time to a checked answer |
 
### The problem
 
*Procurement teams hold contracts with 100–200 suppliers, but the answer to a simple question — what are our payment terms? — can be spread across a master agreement, a statement of work, later amendments and price schedules. Checking one term by hand takes about ten minutes: find the document, make sure no amendment changed it, then find the exact clause. So people skip it — and invoices get overpaid, early-payment discounts and renewal deadlines get missed, and expired supplier certificates go unnoticed. A generic chatbot doesn't help: in a contract, a confident wrong answer is worse than none.*
 
### The data
 
*Mostly real, public documents: 58 commercial contracts from the CUAD dataset (Atticus Project), chosen for procurement relevance; 12 SEC EDGAR amendments paired with their original agreements; and 18 public data-processing and service-level agreements from vendors including Google Cloud, Amazon, Atlassian, Stripe, Twilio and HubSpot. I added 70 synthetic price schedules and compliance certificates, generated with a fixed seed, so every file type (PDF, HTML, Word, text) and clause type was covered.*
 
*In total: 158 documents from 69 suppliers. Cleaning stripped headers, footers and redaction marks and standardised dates, so each chunk keeps a stable ID that citations can point to.*
 
### Approach
 
- ***Clause-aware chunking.*** *Contracts are split at clause boundaries, not fixed lengths, so every retrieved piece is a complete clause a citation can point to.*
- ***Hybrid retrieval.*** *Meaning-based vector search finds paraphrases; keyword search (BM25) finds exact terms like "Net 45" that embeddings can blur. The two ranked lists are merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, then a cross-encoder re-ranks the top results.*
- ***Local embeddings.*** *Embeddings run on the machine instead of through an API. With 158 documents this costs nothing and keeps the free-tier model quota for answering questions.*
- ***Amendments follow their originals.*** *When an amendment is retrieved, the clause it changes in the original agreement comes with it, and vice versa, up to three links deep. In the example below, Amendment 1 replaces the payment period: the answer gives the new 45 days and says which clause it replaced.*
- ***Refuse before guessing.*** *If the best match scores below a tuned relevance threshold, the system refuses before calling the language model at all — no answer is invented, and no quota is spent.*
- ***Check every answer.*** *After an answer is written, code — not the model — confirms that every citation exists in the documents and every number appears in the cited clause. Passing answers get a "Checked" seal; anything else is marked for a person to verify.*
### Integration and debugging
 
*When the two halves of the system first ran together, 3 of 223 tests failed and generation didn't work at all. I led the integration and kept a debugging log of 37 findings — from installation failures and retired model names to answers refused after an amendment upload — and fixed 24 of them. The rest are recorded as open, partly fixed or by design. The log also records why the relevance threshold was re-tuned three times as real questions exposed where it was too strict.*
 
### Evaluation
 
*A labelled set of 67 questions: 30 drawn from real CUAD contracts (renewal, termination, liability, pricing, compliance), 22 exact-value questions (unit prices, payment terms, certificate expiry dates), and 15 questions the documents cannot answer. The set was split into 40 tuning and 27 held-out questions. Measured on the tuning set with real models and caching off: the correct clause was in the top 5 for 29 of 31 answerable questions, all 9 unanswerable questions were refused, and 1 answerable question was wrongly refused.*
 
**Figure:** a checked answer, showing the cited clause and the amendment that changed it (Section 11).
 
### Results
 
*93.5% recall@5, every unanswerable question refused, a 3.2% false-refusal rate, and checked answers in one to five seconds. The final version has a redesigned interface with per-contract conversations, a "Terms found" table showing which clause is in force and since when, and an insights page tracking answer rate and speed against targets.*
 
### Limitations
 
- ***Tuned, not yet held out.*** *All headline figures come from the 40 tuning questions — the same ones used to set the threshold. The 27 held-out questions haven't been run, so the figures may be optimistic.*
- ***Citation accuracy isn't measured.*** *The checks confirm citations exist and numbers match, but whether the cited clause is the right one hasn't been scored by the evaluation judge yet.*
- ***A small evaluation set.*** *Forty questions is a thin basis for a threshold that decides when to refuse.*
- ***Smaller than a real repository.*** *158 documents is a simulation; the price schedules and certificates are synthetic, and performance on a large, messy enterprise collection is untested.*
- ***Fragile when free models fail.*** *If both model providers hit rate limits, a request can take over 40 seconds and end in a generic error. There's no retry or circuit breaker yet.*
- ***Upload order matters.*** *An amendment uploaded before its original agreement isn't linked to it.*
### What I'd do next
 
1. ***Run the held-out set*** *and re-check the threshold on questions it was never tuned on.*
2. ***Measure citation accuracy*** *with the evaluation judge, since it's what users actually trust.*
3. ***Compare each retriever on its own*** *— vector only, keyword only and hybrid — to show what each part contributes.*
4. ***Link amendments automatically*** *by supplier and date, so upload order stops mattering.*
5. ***Add retries and clear errors*** *for when model providers are rate-limited.*
## 5. Case study: ChargebackLens
 
### Header
 
- **Label:** *Payments & fintech risk · Aug–Sep 2026*
- **Title:** *ChargebackLens — Transaction Dispute Risk & Decision Engine*
- **Summary:** *A calibrated risk model that turns each transaction's chance of being disputed into the action that loses the least money — allow it, ask for extra verification, or send it for manual review.*
- **Details:** *Role: solo project · Built for the Razorpay AI Buildathon, Track 02: AI Risk Manager · Duration: Aug–Sep 2026*
- **Stack:** *Python, NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-learn, Plotly, Streamlit*
- **Links:** *View code* → [GitHub](https://github.com/Inquisitiveshri34/ChargeBackLens-Transaction-Dispute-Risk-Decision-Engine) · *Try the app* → [Live demo](https://chargebacklens-transaction-dispute-risk-decision-engine.streamlit.app/)
### At a glance
 
All figures are on the held-out test period: 45,246 transactions, 407 disputes (a 0.90% dispute rate).
 
| Number | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 9.7× | PR-AUC of 0.0872 against a 0.90% base rate — the model ranks disputes about 9.7 times better than chance |
| 16.2× lift | 66 disputes among the 452 highest-risk transactions (top 1%) |
| 43.2% recall at 5% reviewed | Reviewing the riskiest 5% (2,262 transactions) catches 175 of the 407 disputes |
| ₹11.99L net savings | 39.1% of the ₹30.7L that allowing every transaction would cost, under assumed costs — see Limitations |
| +₹1.19L from the third band | Three bands save ₹11.99L; the best single threshold saves ₹10.81L |
| 0.00185 calibration error | Predicted risks closely match how often disputes actually happen |
 
### The problem
 
*A merchant loses money to a chargeback in three ways: the disputed amount, a fixed dispute fee, and the time spent handling it. But stopping a genuine customer costs money too — someone asked for extra verification may simply abandon the purchase. Most fraud models only ask "is this risky?" The question a merchant actually faces is "which action loses the least money?" ChargebackLens is built to answer that.*
 
### The data
 
*Synthetic data for Indian payments: 120,000 transactions from 35,000 customers and 60 merchants, spread across five files — transactions, customers, merchants, fulfilment and disputes. I fixed 14 kinds of data issues, such as non-standard state codes, mismatched delivery timestamps and orphaned records, logged each fix against a written specification, and checked the result with 20 automated data-quality checks, all passing. The final table holds 119,988 transactions, of which 1,050 (0.875%) were disputed.*
 
### Approach
 
- ***Three bands instead of one cut-off.*** *Transactions are allowed, stepped up to 3-D Secure verification, or sent for manual review, with both thresholds optimised together. Alternative: a single flag-or-allow threshold. The third band added ₹1.19L in net savings — so the extra complexity is measured, not assumed.*
- ***Refusing information the model couldn't have.*** *I tagged 12 columns as forbidden because they're only known after the decision — such as delivery status, which shows a real 3.1× difference in dispute rates. The feature-building notebook never loads the fulfilment file, so the rule is enforced by design, not by a filter that could be forgotten.*
- ***A time-based split.*** *The model is trained on earlier transactions, chosen and calibrated on a later block, and tested on the latest period — the way it would be used in practice.*
- ***Logistic regression over gradient boosting.*** *I compared several candidates, including a tuned gradient-boosting model. None ranked disputes meaningfully better than another, so I broke the tie on log-loss, which rewards trustworthy probabilities. A plain logistic regression won and is simpler to explain and maintain.*
- ***Sigmoid calibration instead of isotonic.*** *Isotonic calibration is a step function that can merge different scores and blur the top of the review queue; cross-validated calibration would also mix time periods. Sigmoid calibration on one held-out time block keeps the ranking intact.*
- ***Testing the assumptions, not just the model.*** *I varied each of the four cost assumptions to see how the thresholds and savings moved. Savings stayed positive in every case, but one assumption — how many customers abandon when asked to verify — shifts the step-up threshold about 3×, so it's the one number a merchant must measure.*
### Interactive: try the thresholds
 
- **Heading:** *Try the thresholds yourself*
- **Intro:** *Move the two thresholds to see how many transactions are allowed, challenged or reviewed — and what that does to disputes caught and money saved.*
- **Controls:** *Step-up threshold* · *Review threshold* · *Reset to chosen policy*
- **Outputs:** *Allowed* · *Stepped up* · *Reviewed* · *Disputes caught* · *Net savings*
- **Chosen policy (shown on reset):** *93.2% allowed, 6.2% stepped up, 0.7% reviewed — catching 49.9% of disputes (203 of 407) and saving ₹11.99L.*
- **Footnote:** *Computed on the held-out test period (45,246 transactions, 0.90% dispute rate). Assumed costs: ₹1,500 fee per dispute, ₹300 per manual review, an 18% merchant margin, and 25% of customers abandoning when asked to verify.*
### Evaluation
 
*Candidates were compared on a separate validation block (14,947 transactions, 128 disputes) using bootstrapped PR-AUC, with ties broken on log-loss. The chosen model was then scored once on the test period using PR-AUC, lift and recall at fixed review volumes, calibration error across ten equal-sized groups, and a Brier skill score of 0.039 against simply predicting the average dispute rate. The money figures count the real cost of every outcome — caught, missed, or a genuine customer inconvenienced.*
 
**Figures:** four figures — predicted vs actual risk, net savings across thresholds, savings by segment, and the excluded delivery-status signal. Files, captions and alt text are in Section 11.5.
 
### Results
 
*A PR-AUC 9.7 times the base rate, 16.2× lift in the top 1%, and 43.2% of disputes caught by reviewing 5% of transactions. The chosen three-band policy catches 49.9% of disputes while letting 93.2% of transactions through untouched, for ₹11.99L net savings. By segment, travel transactions return about ₹1,094 per intervention, while manual review on wallet payments loses money (₹300 spent, nothing caught) — so the policy recommends skipping human review for low-value segments and keeping only the verification step there. Shipped as a Streamlit app with Score, Review Queue and Economics Explorer views.*
 
### Limitations
 
- ***Synthetic data.*** *The generator didn't model where customers or merchants are based, so location features carry no real signal. Patterns in real payments data would differ.*
- ***The savings figure includes disputes raised after the cut-off date.*** *72.2% of test disputes were raised after the dataset's stated observation date. The ranking isn't affected, but the rupee figures assume knowledge an operator on that date wouldn't yet have. This is the biggest caveat on ₹11.99L.*
- ***A small basis for choosing the model.*** *Only 128 disputes were in the validation block, and the candidates were statistically tied on ranking. Choosing the simpler model is sound, but the evidence separating them is thin.*
- ***Key costs are assumed, not measured.*** *The 25% verification-abandon rate, ₹300 review cost and 18% margin are assumptions. The abandon rate in particular moves the step-up threshold about threefold.*
- ***A demo, not a production system.*** *The app reads saved results; there's no live transaction stream, latency target, drift monitoring or model versioning.*
### What I'd do next
 
1. ***Measure the verification-abandon rate on real traffic*** *— an A/B test of the 3-D Secure step would ground the threshold that matters most.*
2. ***Monitor the score distribution*** *weekly, so thresholds are re-set before the model's behaviour drifts.*
3. ***Find an early, decision-time proxy for fulfilment risk*** *— a signal like a missed processing deadline could capture some of the delivery-status pattern without using information from the future.*
4. ***Break segment economics down to individual merchants*** *so routing can be tuned where it pays most.*
5. ***Replace assumed costs with real merchant data*** *— actual margins and review costs by category.*
## 6. Case study: DeliverIQ
 
### Header
 
- **Label:** *Logistics · Apr–May 2026*
- **Title:** *DeliverIQ — Delivery ETA Predictor*
- **Summary:** *A machine-learning model that predicts urban delivery times from traffic, weather and road-network data, served through an interactive forecasting dashboard.*
- **Details:** Role: solo project · *Duration: Apr–May 2026*
- **Stack:** *Python, NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-learn, Plotly, Streamlit, Jupyter Notebook*
- **Links:** *View code* → [GitHub](https://github.com/Inquisitiveshri34/DeliverIQ-Travel-Time-Predictor) · *Try the app* → [Live demo](https://deliveriq-v0.streamlit.app/)
### At a glance
 
| Number | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 7.83 min MAE | Average prediction error on 47,984 held-out trips |
| 55% better | Than always predicting the average trip time, which is off by 17.48 min on average |
| 239,919 trips | Used after cleaning, from 250,000 synthetic delivery trips |
| R² 0.21 | Kept low by deliberate noise built into the synthetic data — see Limitations |
 
### The problem
 
*A delivery estimate is a promise. Overestimate it and drivers sit idle; underestimate it and customers are let down. City deliveries depend on distance, road type, traffic and weather all at once, which a simple distance-based estimate can't capture.*
 
### The data
 
*A synthetic, multi-table transportation dataset: 250,000 delivery trips joined with 25,000 road segments, 100,000 traffic snapshots and 75,000 weather events. The data was built with deliberate errors — typos, out-of-range values, duplicates, broken references and noise — so cleaning was a real part of the work.*
 
*I standardised category spellings, clipped values to valid ranges (for example, speeds of 1–120 km/h), merged duplicate weather events (75,000 → 37,900), checked every foreign key and filled gaps with group-wise medians or modes, and removed 10,081 trips with no recorded travel time, leaving 239,919. Travel times were heavily skewed (skewness 8.47), so I modelled them on a log scale.*
 
### Approach
 
- ***Log-scale target.*** *I predicted log(1 + minutes) instead of raw minutes. With a skewness of 8.47, raw times would let a few very long trips dominate training.*
- ***Group-wise filling of gaps.*** *Missing values were filled with the median or mode for their road type, traffic level or weather, not one global value, because a highway and a residential street behave very differently.*
- ***One end-to-end pipeline.*** *Scaling and encoding sit inside a single Scikit-learn pipeline, so they're learned only from training data. That prevents information from the test set leaking into the model.*
- ***A step-by-step model progression.*** *Mean baseline → linear regression (18% better) → decision tree tuned with GridSearchCV over 60 combinations (55.3% better) → Random Forest (55.2% better). I chose the Random Forest despite near-identical error, because an ensemble generalises more reliably than one deep tree. Its settings were chosen by hand, informed by the tree search, because a randomised search ran out of memory.*
- ***MAE as the main metric.*** *Dispatchers care about the typical error in minutes. RMSE (35.7 min) is far higher than MAE, which shows a few extreme trips dominate squared error.*
### Evaluation
 
*An 80/20 random split — 191,935 trips for training, 47,984 for testing. The baseline always predicts the training set's average trip time. All metrics were re-computed in a separate verification notebook and matched exactly.*
 
**Figures:**
 
- *Travel times are heavily right-skewed (mean 21.8 min, median 12.3, skewness 8.47); on a log scale they become close to bell-shaped, which makes them usable for modelling.*
- *Road type is a strong signal: median trip times are 6.5 minutes on highways, 12.3 on urban roads and 19.0 on residential streets.*
- *Average travel time by hour and day of week: the 7–9 am and 5–7 pm rush hours stand out on every day of the week.*
- *Speed falls as traffic rises — about 62.5 km/h in low traffic, 40 in medium and 20 in high.*
- *Road type and weather together: average trips range from about 15 minutes on highways in clear weather to about 35 minutes on residential streets in fog.*
### Results
 
*7.83 minutes mean absolute error against 17.48 for the baseline — a 55.2% improvement. Distance is the dominant feature, at 65% of the model's importance. RMSE is 35.7 minutes, R² 0.21 and MAPE 37.8%. Deployed as an interactive Streamlit dashboard for on-demand forecasts.*
 
### Limitations
 
- ***Synthetic data with deliberate noise.*** *Built-in noise and broken references cap how much variation any model can explain (R² 0.21). How it would perform on real deliveries is untested.*
- ***Random rather than time-ordered split.*** *Test trips come from the same period as training trips, so the score doesn't show how well the model predicts future weeks.*
- ***Impossible distances left in the data.*** *Some trips list distances of hundreds to thousands of kilometres yet take under 90 minutes. Cleaning didn't cap distance, so these errors reached the model.*
- ***A spike of trips at midnight.*** *About 21,600 trips are logged at 00:00, more than twice any other hour, which suggests missing times were filled in as midnight. That weakens the hour and rush-hour features.*
- ***Large errors on rare long trips.*** *A few trips run up to about 600 minutes; these drive RMSE to 35.7 minutes even though typical error is under 8.*
- ***Broken road references.*** *13.5% of trips (33,793) had no valid road record and defaulted to "residential", which weakens predictions for those trips.*
- ***No seasonal or holiday effects.*** *Hour and day of week are used, but monthly patterns and holidays are not.*
### What I'd do next
 
1. ***Fix the two data issues and re-measure*** *— cap impossible distances, treat midnight timestamps as missing, and re-run the evaluation.*
2. ***Validate on time-ordered data*** *— train on earlier months and test on later ones, to measure performance on the future.*
3. ***Predict ranges, not single numbers*** *— quantile regression would give dispatchers a likely window, such as 12–18 minutes.*
4. ***Add seasonal and holiday features*** *to capture monthly and festival-period patterns.*
5. ***Monitor drift after deployment*** *— compare predictions with actual times and retrain when error rises.*

### 7.1 StreakForge (featured)
 
- **Label:** *Product analytics · Aug–Sep 2026*
- **Title:** *StreakForge — Fitness Engagement & Retention Insight Platform*
- **Summary:** *An insight platform that shows a gym chain's product team which engagement behaviours actually drive retention — and found that the biggest leak is members who never get started, not members who stop renewing.*
- **Details:** *Team project of three. I wrote the product requirements document and led the exploratory analysis, KPI design, insight storytelling and dashboard delivery across 10 pull requests. Teammates built the data cleaning, merging and feature-engineering pipeline.*
- **Stack:** *Python, NumPy, Pandas, Plotly, Scikit-learn, Streamlit*
- **Links:** [GitHub](https://github.com/kalviumcommunity/JDF_StreakForge_Kalvium-Community) · [Live demo](https://streakforge-fitness-engagement-retention-insight-platform.streamlit.app/)
**At a glance**
 
| Number | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 81.2% | of members never activate — no visit or app session in their first 7 days. The single largest drop in the funnel. |
| 94% → 84% | Headline churn once it's measured at the date the data actually ends |
| 16% → 24% | Outside January, activation sat at 15–16% every year from 2021 to 2024, then rose to 23.7% in 2025 — the leak is real but shrinking |
| 0 of 40 | Branches whose churn rate is a statistical outlier — the problem is platform-wide, not a few bad branches |
| 16,956 members | From five sources totalling 309,497 rows, merged into 206,732 engagement events |
| 15 KPIs · 13 unusual weeks · 12 at-risk members | Defined, flagged and listed for outreach |
 
**The problem**
 
*A gym chain with a companion app runs 40 branches across Indian cities. Its engagement data sits in five separate systems — member profiles, subscriptions, streaks, gym check-ins and app events — with no shared structure or consistent timestamps. The product team can see that members leave, but not which behaviours keep them. Retention spending is guesswork.*
 
**The data**
 
*A synthetic dataset designed around realistic Indian gym patterns — city-tier pricing, festival and monsoon seasonality, corporate tie-ups — and generated with deliberate messiness. Five sources totalling 309,497 rows: 17,500 members, 45,088 subscriptions, 40,177 streak episodes, 108,744 gym check-ins and 97,988 app events.*
 
*The team's cleaning removed 676 duplicate subscriptions and 25 duplicate streaks, parsed two conflicting date formats, and collapsed 81 spellings of city names into 27 cities (Bangalore and Bengaluru, Gurgaon and Gurugram). Missing values were kept as meaning rather than filled: a missing trainer means a self-guided session.*
 
**Approach**
 
- ***One timeline of events.*** *Our pipeline combined gym check-ins and app events into a single table of 206,732 events and tagged each event with the member's subscription and streak status at that exact moment. Alternative: analysing gym and app data separately, or summarising straight to one row per member — both lose the timing that churn and activation depend on. Eleven merge checks confirmed no rows were duplicated or lost.*
- ***Measuring members once, not once per event.*** *In my analysis, churn, spend and tenure were computed from the member tables, not the event table. Otherwise an active member with 30 events would count 30 times and skew every average.*
- ***A funnel where each stage builds on the last.*** *Signed up → activated in 7 days → renewed → 7-day streak → retained. Because the stages cascade, each drop is a true sequential loss, which is what isolated activation as the main leak.*
- ***Questioning the headline number.*** *The pipeline's reporting date sat ten months after the last real event, so members looked churned simply because the data had stopped. I computed every KPI at both dates and reported both side by side: 94% churn at the reporting date, 84% at the data's real end.*
- ***Rule-based segments, not clustering.*** *Members were scored on recency, frequency and spend in quartiles and placed in four segments — Champions, Loyal, At Risk, Dormant. A manager can see why a member is "At Risk"; a cluster label can't explain itself.*
- ***Anomalies at platform level.*** *Comparing each week with its 8-week rolling average flagged 13 unusual weeks — the largest are New Year sign-up rushes. A 14th, the partial final week of data, was recognised as an artefact and excluded rather than reported as a spike. A separate check on churn by branch found no outliers among 40 branches.*
**What I found**
 
- *Churn doesn't depend on who the member is. It was about 94% in every segment — membership type, city tier, signup channel, even "Champions" versus "Dormant" — and no category was meaningfully associated with churn (Cramér's V below 0.1).*
- *What matters is getting started. Only 18.8% of members activate in their first week. That single step loses 13,776 members — more than every later stage combined.*
- *Guided plans help members start: activation was 27.9% on PT-Combo plans against 15.6% on Basic, and 23.5% with a personal trainer against 17.6% without — the only statistically significant differences. Activation also improved: outside January it held at 15–16% for four years, then rose to 23.7% in 2025. January sign-ups activate best every year.*
- *Twelve currently active members show almost every risk signal at once, and were listed for outreach.*
**Results**
 
*A deployed six-page Streamlit dashboard — cached loading, filters, error handling, health checks and export — presenting 15 KPIs and five insights, each routed to the audience that should act on it (leadership, product, operations or customer success), across seven interactive charts.*
 
**Limitations**
 
- ***Synthetic data.*** *The patterns are designed to be realistic, but the findings show the method, not truths about a real gym chain.*
- ***Churn is inferred from silence.*** *A member counts as churned 30 days after their plan ends without renewal. The data can't separate a deliberate cancellation from a forgotten renewal or a failed payment, and each needs a different response.*
- ***The 7-day activation window wasn't tested.*** *Members who start on day 8 count as not activated. Re-running with 14 or 30 days would change the size of the drop.*
- ***Correlation, not cause.*** *There's no experiment showing that improving activation would reduce churn, and no explanation yet for why activation improved in 2025. The single highest monthly figure, 36.6% for September 2025, sits at the very edge of the data and isn't relied on.*
- ***Uniform churn is a puzzle.*** *"Champions" churning at the same rate as "Dormant" members is a strong finding, but it may partly reflect how the synthetic data was generated.*
**What I'd do next**
 
1. ***Test onboarding changes directly*** *— experiment with the first-week experience (welcome timing, a scheduled first workout) and measure activation against the 2025 baseline of 23.7%. Guided plans are the obvious first lever to test.*
2. ***Find out why activation improved*** *— identify what changed for the cohorts where it rose, so the improvement can be kept.*
3. ***Test the activation window*** *at 7, 14 and 30 days to see whether the main finding holds.*
4. ***Run the same pipeline on real gym data*** *to check whether the activation leak and the uniform churn appear there too.*
5. ***Score at-risk members continuously*** *instead of once, so outreach happens before the 30-day grace period ends.*
